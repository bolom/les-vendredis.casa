## Why Shingles, Why Wood, Why This

This photograph captures a moment mid-project where the transition is visible—old weathered shingles in grey tones giving way to freshly installed reddish-brown shingles. It's not a before-and-after. It's documentation of *becoming*.

## Why Wood in the Tropics?

Modern building science offers countless alternatives to wooden shingles: metal roofing, synthetic composites, engineered materials designed to resist everything from UV to salt spray. These are fine solutions. They're durable, low-maintenance, and engineered.

Yet we chose wood. Here's why:

**Thermal Performance**: Wood shingles create a small air gap when installed (proper installation has gaps for seasonal wood movement). This gap provides insulation and, critically, allows air circulation underneath. In tropical heat, this prevents the roof from becoming an oven. Metal roofing, by contrast, conducts heat directly into the structure.

**Acoustic Character**: During tropical downpours, a metal roof becomes a drum. Rain sounds like violence. Wood absorbs sound, dampens it, makes the same rainfall feel like a peaceful event. This is psychological, but psychology matters.

**Living Material**: Wood weathers in ways that tell stories. The transition from fresh reddish-brown to silver-grey isn't degradation—it's the material interacting with sunlight, rain, salt air, and time. A wooden roof records climate visibly.

**Local Economy**: Properly sourced wooden shingles support local timber operations, craftspeople trained in traditional methods, and economies built around sustainable forestry. A metal roof comes from a factory half a world away.

**Cultural Continuity**: In this region, wooden shingles aren't exotic. They're traditional. Choosing them connects Les Vendredis to architectural lineage, to builders who came before, to methods proven across centuries.

## The Installation: Craft as Method

Shingling isn't something a general contractor dabbles in. It's a specialized skill that requires:

**Understanding wood movement**: Wood moves with moisture and temperature. Shingles must be fastened in ways that allow this movement without creating gaps or buckling. This isn't done by memorization—it's internalized knowledge.

**Pattern discipline**: Every shingle must overlap the one below it by precisely the right amount (typically 5 inches with an 8-inch exposure). Too little overlap and you've invited water infiltration. Too much and you've wasted material and created an uneven line. The discipline of perfect repetition, row after row up a steep pitch, requires intense focus.

**Tool mastery**: Traditional shingling uses techniques older than powered tools. The nail placement, the fastener type, the technique of seating the shingle—these are skills that take years to develop fully. A shingle can be installed "well enough" in minutes or installed *properly* with attention that takes time.

**Reading the material**: Good craftspeople read wood like musicians read a score. This piece is straight-grained, can be fastened near the edge. This one has a knot—shift the fasteners to avoid it. That one has run-off grain—adjust technique. This responsiveness to material is what separates craft from labor.

## The Color Transition

This image is particularly powerful because it shows the transition. The grey shingles (which are actually new, but have been exposed to weather for months already) speak to how quickly wood weathers in a tropical marine environment. The fresh shingles show the color that will exist for perhaps a year before beginning their own journey toward silver.

This isn't a flaw. It's the material's honesty. You're not choosing a color that lasts forever in a plastic sheen. You're choosing a process, a relationship with time and weather.

For some, this is precisely what's appealing: a roof that ages visibly, that doesn't pretend to be permanent, that acknowledges entropy. For others, it's a maintenance commitment—treating the shingles with preservatives to extend the cycle, or accepting the faster weathering as the cost of authenticity.

## The Labor Reality

The visible progression in this image is also documentation of labor. Each shingle requires:

- Positioning
- Assessment for grain and defects
- Fastening (typically with hand-driven hot-dipped galvanized nails)
- Checking for proper seating
- Moving to the next one

On a steep pitch. Often in sun. With safety systems (harness, scaffolding) that add weight and constraint. This isn't the kind of work that can be rushed. And rushing it would be obvious—visible in misaligned rows, inconsistent exposure, fasteners that miss marks.

Good shingling is slow work. That's not a problem. That's a feature.

## Living with Shingles

What the photograph doesn't show—but you'll experience living beneath these shingles—is the sound of a tropical rainstorm. The gentleness of it. The organic rhythm. The way the sound changes as rain intensifies then subsides. The silence after, when water drips from the eaves and creates a smaller, more intimate rhythm.

You'll also experience the maintenance: periodic inspection, treatment with preservative every few years, the occasional replacement of a damaged shingle. This isn't passive shelter. It's engagement with your roof, ongoing relationship with the material.

---

**Shingling is the conversation between hand, material, and time. Every row speaks to choices made by the person placing each shingle—and in that communication, craft becomes visible.**
