## The Economics of Sweetness

This photograph captures that possibility concentrated into two perfect yellow guavas: the promise that land maintained thoughtfully will feed you. Not symbolically. Actually.

## Why Guavas Matter

Guavas are a revealing choice for this property. They're not exotic in the way that, say, dragon fruit or exotic rambutan might be. They're not status fruits. They're practical, productive, and indicative of good soil management.

A mature guava tree produces prolifically. A single tree can yield hundreds of fruits annually. The fruit is:

- **Nutritionally dense**: More vitamin C per ounce than citrus
- **Tolerant**: Guavas grow in poor soil, though they produce better with care
- **Productive**: Early bearing (3-4 years from planting), long productive life
- **Versatile**: Eaten fresh, made into paste, juice, jam, or tea from the leaves

The presence of these guavas—clearly visible, clearly accessible, clearly being maintained—speaks to a philosophy: this property isn't a pretend-retreat where you look at nature. It's a place where you participate in it.

## The View from Below

This image is shot looking upward into the canopy—a perspective that emphasizes abundance and height. The blue sky behind creates contrast that makes the golden fruit pop. But there's also something about the low angle that's democratic: this is how you see fruit when you're standing beneath the tree, reaching up, beginning to harvest.

There's no distancing here. No "we have a lovely view of our orchard from the main house" aesthetics. It's intimate engagement with the source of food.

## What Good Soil Creates

The presence of thriving guava trees indicates soil that's been managed. In tropical environments, soil is dynamic—simultaneously alive with biology and vulnerable to leaching. Heavy rains can strip nutrients. Without attention, productivity drops.

The fact that these guavas are plump, well-colored, and clearly being picked at peak ripeness suggests:

- **Soil amendment**: Likely compost, mulch, possibly green manuring
- **Nutrient cycling**: Fallen leaves are being managed, not stripped away
- **Water management**: Either good rainfall distribution or thoughtful irrigation
- **Pest pressure management**: These fruits show no evidence of insect damage despite their exposed position

This is horticulture, not just farming. It's engagement, attention, care.

## Seasonal Markers

In places without frost, how do you know what season it is? In part, by what's ripening. Guava season announces itself. You look at the trees and know: this is the moment. You pick at peak ripeness or you lose the fruit to birds and insects.

For people living at Les Vendredis, these ripening moments will become temporal markers. Not "it's September" but "it's guava season." Not "three months until," but "the avocados are starting to flower."

This rhythm—aligning human activity with biological cycles—is something that modern logistics have largely erased. We eat guavas year-round now, shipped from wherever they're in season. The choice to harvest from your own trees reintroduces this rhythm, this connection between season and action.

## The Ethics of Agriculture Here

This property has a choice: it can be a manicured escape from agriculture, a green space maintained to look natural while actually being highly controlled. Or it can be genuinely productive.

The guavas suggest the latter. They're not ornamental. They're yielding food. They're evidence that this space is in relationship with productivity, with feeding, with the land's capacity to sustain.

This is harder than sterile landscaping. Productive gardens mean insects, management, attention to soil, willingness to harvest and process. It means jam-making rather than storebought, preservation rather than novelty. It means accepting that sometimes the birds get some fruit, and that's okay.

## What You'll Eat

Living at or visiting Les Vendredis in guava season means:

- **Breakfast**: Fresh guava sliced over breakfast, unsurpassably sweet
- **Juice**: Guava juice made from over-ripe fruit (the ones that would otherwise fall)
- **Paste**: Guava paste (guayaba) made by cooking fruit down with sugar, a traditional preserve
- **Tea**: If you steep guava leaves in hot water, you get a mild, pleasant tea
- **Sharing**: The abundance means extra fruit to give to neighbors, friends, workers

This is basic abundance. It's what agricultural land offers when it's maintained: more than you anticipated, more than you need, enough to feel generous and connected to the wider community.

## The Reality of Abundance

The promise of a productive tropical property is real, but it's not passive. You don't plant a guava tree and passively enjoy endless fruit. You plant it, wait for maturity, manage it through pest pressures, harvest when ready, and process before the fruit spoils.

But that's not a downside. That's the *point*. The work of maintaining orchard, of processing harvest, of sharing the surplus—these are the activities that make a retreat functional rather than escapist. These are how you stay connected to cycles larger than yourself.
