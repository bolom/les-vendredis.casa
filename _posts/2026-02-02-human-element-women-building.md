## Progress Looks Like This

This image documents something crucial about Les Vendredis that often gets glossed over in architectural portfolios: the *people* doing the work. Not the concept of people, but actual individuals, risking their bodies, focusing their expertise, and taking pride in craftsmanship that will outlast their involvement in the project.

## Women in High Places

The scaffolding tells its own story. The horizontal wooden planks, bolted and braced with the kind of improvisation that requires deep structural understanding. The safety harness, properly worn and clipped. The power tools, held with the casual competence of someone who knows exactly what they're doing.

In many construction contexts, women remain underrepresented. In traditional carpentry and roofing, even more so. Yet here, in a tropical climate where precision matters and stakes are high, a woman is executing the critical work of shingling—the final defensive layer between the structure and the elements.

The MULTIVAP 200 vapor barrier visible behind her is German engineering. The wooden shingles are local craft. The combination speaks to how a well-executed project draws expertise from wherever it lives.

## Safety as Skill

Notice what *doesn't* appear in the carefree composition: negligence. Every element of safety is present and correct:

- **Harness**: Properly fitted, clipped to anchor point
- **Scaffolding**: Solid wooden construction, tested through previous use
- **Footwear**: Appropriate for grip and support
- **Hand protection**: Gloves visible, protecting against splinters and tool contact
- **Tools**: Professional-grade equipment, not improvised substitutes

When women work in high-risk environments, safety often becomes a point of friction—either over-regulated ("we need extra precautions") or under-supported ("figure it out"). Here, the approach is simply *competent*: appropriate tools, proper training, systems that work.

## The Economics of Craftsmanship

What this image represents economically is the *real cost* of quality. You cannot hire amateur labor to shingle a structure on a tropical mountainside. You need people who understand grain, fastening patterns, weather exposure, and how materials will behave across seasons. That knowledge comes from experience, and experience is expensive.

But it's not an expense—it's an investment. Every properly installed shingle is a small insurance policy against the next hurricane season.

## Beyond Aesthetics

The thumbs-up could be read as simple positivity. In context, it's something more: it's the declaration of someone who knows the work matters, who understands they're part of a legacy of craftsmanship, and who takes legitimate pride in a job executed well.

For Les Vendredis, this isn't a portrait of "having women work on our project." It's documentation of the fact that excellence, at this level, isn't gendered. It's earned through commitment, training, and the willingness to do difficult work well.

**The buildings that last aren't the ones built fastest or cheapest. They're the ones built by people who show up, who know their craft, and who take genuine pride in creating something that will shelter others for decades to come.**
